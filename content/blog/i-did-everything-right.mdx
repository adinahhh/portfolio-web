When I was in college, I went to group therapy sessions with other sexual assault survivors. It was cathartic, eye-opening, and ultimately a safe space for me to share my innermost thoughts with others. I don’t really have a space like that anymore, but I do have thoughts I want to share so I am going to write them here.

### The Layoff
I was laid off at the end of October 2025. It wasn’t shocking, and initially a piece of me felt relief. I felt burnt out and needed some time to give myself a mental rest. I’ve always felt positive I will find a role that is a better fit for me.

I’ve brought that positivity with me to in person networking events. The amount of times I’ve encountered people who immediately discourage me, people who look at me like I’m cuckoo for Cocoa Puffs, people who try to dim my light when all they know is my first name & current job status has been disheartening. 

One comment I see quite a bit on social media during discussions about layoffs is how laid off folks feel like a “shell” of their former selves. Anytime I see that comment I am taken back to my college group therapy sessions. Being assaulted can make you feel like a shell of your former self, too. I also see people discuss how they “did everything right” but were still laid off. Does that not sound like the same rhetoric we see with survivors of SA? I find myself comparing my two lived experiences quite often. 

### The Comparison
It’s an odd comparison to make but both experiences have changed me as a person, for better or worse. How I carry myself, how I talk to myself, how I advocate for myself, and how I walk into rooms has absolutely evolved after getting let go. The psychological aftermath feels very similar. How do I determine what is a safe space? Who can I share my true feelings with?  

Much like breathing a sigh of relief when meeting a fellow SA survivor, I find myself filled with relief when I meet another individual who is currently in between roles. They just get it. No explanations needed. No faking the funk. An immediate sense of camaraderie. I know I can share anything I’m feeling or have experienced with the job market and they will understand.

### Pushing Forward
I had two interviews with one company. The first interview was with a recruiter. I asked him if he had any concerns with my previous experience. I checked all the required boxes for the role, but he brought up how I hadn’t written tickets for juniors on a team before. I hadn’t officially mentored juniors as a senior engineer because my last team did not have any juniors. However, I regularly mentored junior engineers in an unofficial capacity over several years. I helped onboard over 100 entry level engineers and I mentored several engineers 1:1. The recruiter told me further down the interview process, they might choose to down level me based on this. He asked if I would consider the lower level role. In my head, I was thinking about how I don’t currently have an income. I agreed to consider it.

The second interview was with a hiring manager. The interview went well until I realized the questions being asked correlated to the lower level role. The recruiter down leveled me without confirming with me first. The hiring manager looked a bit bewildered. I got a rejection email not too long after. It took me a second to step back, analyze my skills and really, truly acknowledge I am a valuable employee for any team that needs my skillset. I was so focused on finding a role I was willing to accept someone else's assessment of my worth before considering my own. I will not consider a down leveling again if I meet all the requirements of the role I initially apply to.

I went to a cyber event a few months back with a speed networking session. Most participants were actively job searching. During one block, I sat across from someone who wasn’t seeking employment and he asked me “How does it feel seeing your competition in the room?” Without hesitation I responded “Oh, they aren’t my competition.” And I laughed. He looked perplexed and I explained why. My background is in software development. Most of the folks in the room had a cyber background or were entry level. My work experience most likely doesn’t match up with others. I absolutely would not have answered his question with that much confidence pre-layoff.

### Navigating Shame
Both experiences frequently carry feelings of shame. I suppose where my experiences differ is how I’ve navigated shame after losing my job. I have not ever felt shame about getting laid off. I had four positive annual reviews, was promoted twice within four years, and my former employer confirmed publicly the layoff was not performance based. What exactly do I have to be ashamed about? That’s right: nothing.

Sometimes I will meet people and they assume I need words of affirmation. It feels similar to how someone might offer a SA survivor a hug. I have gone to events and seen the universal look of pity after people ask me where I work. While I carry no shame, I do find it’s often placed onto me as a laid off person. I don’t think people have ill intentions when they do this. I believe there is an understanding that most people know layoffs aren’t performance based anymore, but there is still an invisible black mark for folks who have been let go.

### The Aftermath
Sometimes I feel like maybe I was meant to go through this layoff to encourage me to further grow as a person. Now that I’m not working, I’ve started new hobbies to challenge myself. Things I’ve wanted to do for years but put off because work was more important. Now I’m a dancer, a gardener, a writer… Maybe assessing how I’ve changed after being laid off is a full circle moment for me to think back to my younger self and finally heal what I once lost. Do I have more healing to do? If I am still discovering new things about myself through experiences that echo old traumas, was my healing ever truly finished?
